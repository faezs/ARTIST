Fourth pass · everything behind the dish

The secondary on its style, the beam down the stalk, nothing in the light

The complaint that stood since the first message was shading, and the three passes before this one never met it: they kept Hashemi's near method, a focal tube through a slot in the membrane with the strip at its top, and only kept the mount out of the light. That tube shades a tenth of the aperture at el 59, its ring more, the hole and the open slot more again. Here the secondary stands on a conical style rising from the centre hole, inside its own shadow; the beam returns through a hole a quarter the size to an image behind the vertex; one fold on the head turns it along the neck axis, through the hollow flexure trunnion, to a second fold at the stalk that sends it down inside the stalk to the same F2 the underground relay already expects. Two fixed 45-degree folds are possible only because the stalk stands beside the head, not under it: a single fold under the neck would graze at the zenith. The FACT mount is unchanged in kind and moved behind the membrane: a hollow cartwheel pivot for the neck, the diaphragm fine stage for the dish, water for the balance. The self-setup simulation of the next section is still on the third-pass fork and is being moved to this geometry.

Setup · the machine erects itself and tracks · soft-body simulation

How it sets itself up

The brief's first line was a water hose for the frame, actuated like a vine robot, so that setup and tracking are automatic. This is that machine running: the FACT mount of the next section with the hose as its frame, as a 3-D soft-body simulation in NVIDIA Warp. Two fabric posts on the deck ring, two fabric arms and two counterweight tubes on the cradle are pressurised and grown from stubs the way an everting vine grows; water is pumped into the counterweight tubes; a pneumatic strut per side turns the cradle about the flexure trunnions on the axis through F to the morning sun and follow it through the day, while the deck ring turns the azimuth. Nothing is placed by hand. The dish rides on the flexure fine stage of sheets 38-39, closed on the sun once the fork has arrived, so the film shows the cradle wandering by degrees and the dish holding to a fraction of a degree; the microradians are the locked stage's stiffness in the title block. The first attempt at this section, a single vine stem from a root on the deck with a bridle to F (the second pass), failed in the simulation for a geometric reason the memo records: one stem from a fixed root cannot give the head both its place on the sphere about F and its sun-pointing attitude, because the two are coupled through the stem's curvature; the fork's rotation about the axis through F gives both at once. The vine-robot theses and the inflated-beam collapse models the tubes are checked against are in the memo; the tracking data feeds a Koopman model (pykoopman) for the controller that replaces the hand-tuned loops.

The mount · synthesized by FACT · third pass

Blade trunnions on the axis through F, a cradle, a diaphragm fine stage

Hopkins's six steps (2010, Fig. 2.7) run to the end for the Cassegrain Hashemi machine of the nix-support fork; the optics are theirs and untouched, and everything here is behind or beside the light. The elevation rotation about the horizontal through F is made by cross-blade cells whose planes contain that axis. Because the constraint space of a rotation is the same set of lines everywhere along its axis, the cells sit where the axis leaves the dish's shadow: two trunnion blocks 6.5 m apart on a fork from the deck ring, with the wind's moment about F arriving as a pair of forces. The dish rides in a cradle on the blocks; a flexure diaphragm between cradle and dish holds it to microradians. A first draft of this pass put one block at F with struts to the dish: it shaded 2.9 % and the crosswind's moment overloaded its blades; the memo keeps both faults beside the correction.

The flower · the same machine, organ by organ

How this is a flower

The brief asked for the structural dual of Hashemi's machine: his dish rides a rail on the focal circle, ours swings on a radius about F; his structure stands around the dish, ours below and beside it, a head on a stalk. Read organ by organ it is a heliotropic bowl flower with an inferior ovary, the plant that already does this job: Arctic poppies and Dryas turn their parabolic corollas to the sun and warm the gynoecium at the focus (Kevan 1975), a pulvinus turns a leaf by moving water between its cells, the sunflower's ovary sits below the corolla. The table gives the correspondence with its numbers; the drawing and the recoloured model follow. It is not a flower in silhouette: the stalk is a fork of two posts, because the flexure pivot needs two blocks far apart to turn the crosswind's moment into forces.

Structures · directionally compliant lattices at full scale

Second pass and the pneumatic mount: the record

After Shaw, Sun, Portela, Barranco, Greer and Hopkins (2019): a block is sliced into rigid layers and every interface cell is bridged by flexure elements whose lines lie in the constraint space of the desired freedom, so each interface allows exactly that freedom and the stack multiplies the range. The screw-algebra check and a frame finite-element model give exactness, stiffness ratio, torque, modes, stress and thermal drift. Models are rotatable below.

First pass · catalogue elements, superseded

Mirror 1 · primary pumped-membrane dish, a = 2.10 m

Pitch, figure and carriage on Hashemi's fixed-focus mount

Kept for the numbers in their title blocks; the topologies above replace them. The dish orbits the focus at one focal length with its attitude slaved to a focus-centred arc. Sliding the fold's shadow off the dish needs a β/2 pitch the carriage does not have; correcting the sphere's off-axis astigmatism needs the rim to become a weak toroid. Both are flexures here. The rollers and tow-wire keep rolling parts only where a 6.6 m travel forces them.

First pass · superseded

Mirror 2 · beam-down fold at the focus F

The mirror hangs face-down at F inside a fixed hood; its normal bisects the incoming beam and the vertical, so the tilt is 45° − el_b/2 and the azimuth comes free from the carriage beam. The flexures sit on the back and never see the beam. Gravity preloads the chain, and the unpowered state is the parked, beam-dumped one.

First pass · superseded

Mirror 3 · M5 ellipsoidal relay, 43 facets

Each facet stands on three folded bipod feet riding parallel-motion carriages: an exact constraint that is athermal by symmetry, adjusted by hard-contact detent screws so no held position depends on a stressed flexure. The design finding that changed the build: the facets must be pressed toroids, not rolled strips.
